PunchApp

A strike the whole queue can read. A hit the striker gets to keep.

Nobody left the house planning to do this.

Walk-up mall traffic. Groups, queues, under five seconds per turn.

The chapters: The problem · The idea · The machine · The phone · The file

A column of glass on the ground floor.

A 1080 by 3840 portrait screen, one and a half metres of glass at Dubai Mall by the Ice Rink, running all day. One credit buys one attempt: a sensor turns the strike into a score and a camera records it.

Read from a metre by the player, and from three by the queue. Nothing can rely on sound, and nobody is standing by to help.

Who is standing in front of it.

150 to 190 cm tall, from everywhere, sometimes queuing. The column has to work for all of them, no stooping, no reaching.

From the back, it's a poster. A taller bar reads. Six digits don't.

The column is the screen.

A strike sends the charge up the glass, and it climbs only as high as the hit deserves.

Height does the ranking.

On the way up it breaks today's line and friends' marks, so the queue has the verdict before anyone reads a digit.

Then the score lands.

The charge holds while the number drops onto the plate. Scores run to 999,999.000, the decimals smaller so the whole points read first.

One column. One moment at a time.

Every state ends on a timer, a credit, a strike or a server event, so the glass never waits for a touch. This is the real screen, live in the page.

Between players, the leaderboard.

The queue always has a score to beat. Between visits the glass rests on Home.

Scan to connect.

A code at thumb height links a phone before the turn, so the machine knows whose hit is next.

Punch now.

The glass keeps the only clock, twenty seconds with a ring that drains. The phone shows no timer, it says look up.

Reading the strike.

Two seconds between the hit and the number, so the striker is looking up when the score lands.

The big score.

The number at full width for the back of the queue, with the punch itself looping under it.

The result.

Nine sections: the score and its ranks, the replay, the punches other people landed here, the sponsor. A record gets a red moment of its own first, and all end on the one red call, punch again.

From the glass to your pocket.

Friends take turns, so the run is the unit. One code stays put for the whole run, a still of every hit waits on it, and each person keeps their own with one tap.

Scan.

The code opens the app, or its download, and links the phone to this machine.

Connect, or buy credits first.

Connecting spends a credit. An empty wallet opens a pack of three: Apple Pay, Google Pay, a card or PayPal. A failed payment gets a page of its own, saying first that nothing was charged.

Punch.

The phone names the machine, holds the credit, then gets out of the way. No timer here, only look up.

Your hit.

The score lands here too, with the replay, the strike at about 315 kg of peak force, and the boards it just joined: this machine, then the city and the country.

The feed, and a reel of your own.

Other people's hits to watch and react to, and your own kept ones: score, video, when and where. Kept hits start private.

The two together.

The phone hands the turn over and the glass keeps the time. The Animation tab plays them live, and holds a clip of each: the score reveal on the glass, the run on the phone, both recorded from the showcase itself.

What ships is one Figma file.

The system and both surfaces are delivered in the project's Figma file, built natively: every layer is a frame, text or a vector, not a screenshot among them, and all of it on real auto layout.

Arena, the default.

Big Shoulders Display for numbers, Barlow for text, Barlow Condensed for labels, on a warm ink with one red: the charge, the replay's progress and punch again, and nothing else.

Colour is a variable, in four modes.

Arena dark, Arena light, Reference dark, Reference light. Daylight turns the ground and nothing else, and no frame is redrawn for it.

Reference, as first drawn.

The original frames kept as a mode, so every refinement can be compared with where it started.

The decisions, and where I pushed back.

No touch, no audio.

The player has just used both hands and the mall is loud. Anything that needs a finger happens on the phone.

The run is the unit.

Groups take turns, so the run gets one code and a still of every hit. A face stays blurred until its owner keeps it.

The glass keeps to the room.

It names the people in this run, today's crown and the punches landed here. City and country ranks live on the phone.

Four things in the brief I would change.

  • Touch is left undecided. I designed for none.
  • The panel is narrower than stated: at this height the glass is about 0.42 m wide.
  • Three decimals are false precision from the queue. They stay small and quiet, and one switch drops them.
  • One attempt and a walk-away counts the wrong thing. I would count kept hits per run.

What I would put to real players.

Can the queue read who beat whom? Does the code scan in glare from a step back? Does the striker look up in time for the landing?

How AI was used.

Claude in Claude Code worked as a design team, and every step had a reviewer. The brief asked where it was used and what changed afterwards, so both are here.

  • An audit, then five directions.

    The brief audited before any direction, five directions scored by judges working apart, then a spec and three skeptics who reshaped the work around the run.

  • Builders on a shared contract.

    Agents built the Figma file through its API, rendered the reveal from code and gathered the photo library.

  • A panel, then critics.

    Sensor science, arcade design, social play and information design on what the glass should show instead of four ranks and four physics readings, and a critic on every screen.

What I changed afterwards.

  • One clock, and a beat before the number.

    The phone lost its timer, and a reading screen now sits between the strike and the score.

  • Sections, not screens.

    Whole-screen alternatives became sections with five designs each, so one part of a page can change alone.

  • The strike in numbers.

    Energy in kJ and speed in mph were units nobody here uses, two of them impossible. Force in kg and speed in km/h replaced them.

  • Red, not gold, and a real sponsor.

    Crowns and records took the brand red, the big score plays the punch itself, and Monster Energy took the sponsor slot in its own green.

  • Then a pass that only cut.

    Out went the phone's saved lists, the carousel controls, a header, a dock and twenty designs that only repeated one another. The result opens on centred score, card grid, list and slant cut, its kinematic breakdown off.
